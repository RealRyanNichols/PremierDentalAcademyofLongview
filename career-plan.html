🔧 Plan 1 — The Hands-On Credential path

The direction, honestly

This path trades the long lecture route for short, skills-first training that ends in a credential employers recognize — dental assisting, welding, HVAC, electrical, medical assisting, cosmetology, CDL driving. Programs are usually measured in weeks or months, not years, and most of the learning happens with tools in your hands.

What's genuinely good about it: you find out fast whether you like the work, you start earning sooner, and the cost of being wrong is a season — not four years and a loan balance. Skills also compound: many people stack credentials over time instead of betting everything on one up front.

What nobody puts in the brochure: the work is physical and you'll feel it; entry-level pay is entry-level, and growth comes from experience and added credentials, not automatically; and quality varies a LOT between programs — a credential from a program employers don't respect is close to worthless. Vetting the school matters as much as picking the trade (our red-flags guide applies to any trade school, not just ours).

Who it fits: people who think with their hands, who want finished results they can point to, and for whom classroom-style school was the price they paid to get to shop class. If lectures drained you but labs woke you up, take this plan seriously.

Who it doesn't: if you loved the academic side of school and want a career that requires a degree (nursing, engineering, teaching), don't let anyone — including a trade school — talk you out of Plan 3.

📅 Test it cheap — one week

  1. Day 1: Pick your top TWO trades (not one — you want a comparison). Write one sentence each on why.
  2. Day 2: For each, find one real person doing it — a family friend, a neighbor, the assistant at your own dentist's office. Ask for 15 minutes or a shadow visit. Most people say yes to a polite, specific ask.
  3. Day 3: Look up the real training requirements from an official source, not ads. For dental assisting in Texas that's the state dental board at tsbde.texas.gov; other trades have their own licensing boards.
  4. Day 4: Sample the actual study material. If dental assisting made your list, our free practice exam and is-it-for-me quiz show you exactly what the material feels like — free, no login.
  5. Day 5: Visit somewhere the work happens. Tour a campus (ours is free to tour), walk a job site, or sit in a shop. The room tells you things a website can't.
  6. Day 6: Price it for real: program cost, gear, licensing fees, and the weeks you won't be earning much. Then check what funding exists — start with our funding finder, which walks you through Workforce Solutions East Texas and other real programs.
  7. Day 7: Do the decision worksheet below with everything you learned. Wrong answers discovered this week are a win — that's the whole point.

🤝 Plan 2 — The People-Facing Skill path

The direction, honestly

Your energy comes from humans — you read rooms, people relax around you, and the best part of school was the people. That's a real professional asset. It's also, on its own, not a career. "People person" becomes a paycheck when it's attached to a skill: a thing you can do that someone will pay for, delivered in a people-facing way.

The move: pick the craft first, then let your people-strength be your multiplier. Fields where care meets craft: dental assisting (half chairside care, half technical skill), medical assisting, patient coordination and front-office healthcare roles, pharmacy tech, teaching-adjacent roles like paraprofessional or child care, hospitality management. In every one, the credential gets you in the door and the people skill gets you promoted.

What nobody tells you: people-facing work spends your emotional energy — the same patients who make the day meaningful can make it exhausting, and you'll meet them on their worst days too. Also, watch out for "people jobs" with no skill attached; they tend to be the lowest-paid and most replaceable roles in any building. The craft is what protects you.

Who it fits: people who genuinely refill on human contact and want to see the person they helped walk out better. Who it doesn't: if a full day of people sounds draining and you'd rather solve the problem than talk it through, look hard at Plans 1 and 3 instead — there's no shame in that, and better to know now.

📅 Test it cheap — one week

  1. Day 1: List three people-facing roles that also carry a skill (from the list above or your own). For each, write what the skill is — if you can't name it, that's your warning.
  2. Day 2: Use the shadowing script below to ask for one visit. Your own dentist, doctor, pharmacist, or a teacher you trust is the easiest yes you'll ever get.
  3. Day 3: Look up real pay for each role on bls.gov — starting pay, not averages, and with the honest caveat that pay varies by employer, experience, and city. For dental assisting in East Texas, our salary page shows how to read local numbers.
  4. Day 4: Do the shadow visit. Watch the worst part of the job on purpose — the scheduling mess, the anxious patient, the cleanup. The best parts sell themselves; the worst parts make the decision.
  5. Day 5: Try the technical half. If dental assisting is on your list, our free practice exam and is-it-for-me quiz sample it honestly.
  6. Day 6: Ask your shadow host one closing question by text: "What separates the people who last in this job from the people who don't?" Save the answer.
  7. Day 7: Decision worksheet below. If two roles tied, the tiebreaker is the skill you'd rather practice on a bad day.
The shadowing script (works by text or in person):
"Hi — I'm [name], I'm a senior at [school] / I just graduated, and I'm seriously considering [role]. Could I watch you work for an hour or two some afternoon, or buy you a coffee and ask a few questions? I'm trying to test this before I commit to training, and you're the person I most want to learn from."
Specific + flattering + low-commitment = yes. If they say no, they'll usually name someone who'll say yes.

📚 Plan 3 — The Academic path, on purpose

The direction, honestly

Let's say it clearly, from a trade school: college is genuinely the right call for some people — and if deep reading, writing, and analysis are where you come alive, you may be one of them. Careers like nursing, engineering, education, accounting, and law require the degree; there is no shortcut into them, and nobody should talk you out of a destination you actually want.

The one rule of this path: go on purpose. The expensive mistake isn't college — it's undecided at college prices: enrolling to defer the decision, borrowing to fund it, and discovering in year three that you were buying time, not education. A degree with a destination is an investment; a degree as a waiting room is the most expensive way to wait ever invented.

What nobody tells you at the college fair: the sticker price and the real price differ at every school, so you have to price YOUR offer, not the average; where you start matters less than finishing — transfer routes through community college are a legitimate, cheaper on-ramp to the same diploma; and changing majors late is one of the most common and costly detours, which is exactly what the destination checklist below exists to prevent.

Who it fits: you liked the academic part of school itself (not just being good at it), and your target career requires the degree. Who it doesn't: if you're choosing college mainly because it's the default and the people around you expect it — while everything in you wants to build, fix, or care for something real this year — read Plans 1, 2, and 4 before you sign anything.

📅 Test it cheap — one week

  1. Day 1: Write your destination in one sentence: "I'm going to college to become a ______." If the blank won't fill, that's not failure — it's data. The rest of the week is about filling it honestly.
  2. Day 2: Reality-check the destination on bls.gov: what it pays, what it requires, whether it's growing. Then find one person in that career and ask them the shadowing question from Plan 2.
  3. Day 3: Sit in on one real college class this month — most colleges will arrange it if you ask admissions, and a community college intro class in your intended major is the truest sample. Lecture on a Tuesday afternoon is the product; buy after tasting.
  4. Day 4: Price three routes to the same degree: (a) four years at your target school, (b) two years community college + transfer, (c) your in-state option. Use each school's own net-price calculator (every U.S. college is required to have one on its site) — not the sticker price.
  5. Day 5: Fill out the cost-comparison worksheet below. Include living costs and the borrowing total at graduation for each route.
  6. Day 6: Build the two-year backup plan (worksheet below): what you'll do if, at the end of year two, the destination has changed. Deciding this NOW, calmly, is what makes changing course later cheap instead of catastrophic.
  7. Day 7: Decision worksheet with your parents in the room. This is the path where their signature is likely on the paperwork — they've earned a voice.

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent one-pager — the academic path

What your student is considering: a four-year (or longer) degree — chosen on purpose, with a destination career attached, not as the default next step.

What "on purpose" means here: they can finish the sentence "I'm going to become a ______," the career actually requires the degree, they've priced three routes to it using net-price calculators, and there's a written two-year check-in with pre-decided options if the plan changes. If the honest answer to "why college?" turns out to be "everyone expected it" — the other three plans in this workbook are the alternative conversation, and having it at 18 is a gift.

🧱 Plan 4 — The Structured path

The direction, honestly

You run best with clear expectations, a defined mission, and a team that notices whether you showed up. That's not a weakness to outgrow — it's a real operating style, and there are whole paths built for it: military service, registered apprenticeships, and earn-while-you-learn structures like small cohort programs where a schedule and a group carry you through.

Military, honestly: it can provide training, discipline, pay, and education benefits afterward — and it is also a serious commitment you cannot quietly quit, with real risks, moves, and years that belong to the service. The single best move before deciding: talk to people who served and got out, not only recruiters. Ask what they'd redo. A recruiter's job is enrollment; a veteran's stories are the honest brochure.

Apprenticeships, honestly: registered apprenticeships pay you while you train toward a credential — the classic earn-while-you-learn deal. The trade-offs: openings are competitive and specific (you apply to a program, not a vibe), progress is structured over years, and quality depends on the sponsor. The official national finder is linked below.

Structured cohorts, honestly: some training programs are built cohort-style — a small group, a fixed schedule, people who notice when you're absent. Our own classes run that way (8 seats, daytime schedule), and we've watched structure carry students that motivation alone wouldn't have. That's one local example of the shape, not the only one — the shape is the point.

Who it fits: people who thrive on external structure and want a team. Who it doesn't: if being told where to be chafes more than it comforts, a multi-year service commitment is the wrong experiment — test with a shorter structured program first.

📅 Test it cheap — one week

  1. Day 1: Write what "structure" means to you, specifically: a schedule? a team? a mission? someone checking your work? Different needs point to different paths — a cohort program gives schedule + team without a service commitment.
  2. Day 2: Find one veteran (family, church, a teacher who served). Ask: "What did the military give you, what did it cost you, and what would you tell an 18-year-old considering it?" Take notes.
  3. Day 3: Find one current or former apprentice — electricians and plumbers are everywhere and usually happy to talk. Same three questions.
  4. Day 4: Browse real apprenticeship openings within driving distance on apprenticeship.gov. Note what they require and when they open.
  5. Day 5: If the military is seriously on your list, read the education benefits directly at va.gov/education — see the GI Bill notes below — and only then, if still interested, talk to a recruiter with your veteran's questions in hand.
  6. Day 6: Experience a structured cohort in person: tour a small-class program (ours is free to tour — ask what an 8-seat class day actually looks like) or visit any team environment you might join.
  7. Day 7: Decision worksheet below. For the military specifically: add a two-week cooling-off rule before signing anything — real structure includes not deciding under sales pressure.

GI Bill notes, honestly: education benefits for service members are real and substantial — for many veterans they cover significant tuition and support after service. But the details (eligibility, transfer to family, what's covered, service-time requirements) change over time and vary by your situation, so never plan around a number you heard secondhand or from a recruiter's summary. Before you count on any benefit: read it yourself at va.gov/education, confirm what applies to the specific service and term you'd sign for, and have a veteran sanity-check your understanding. "Free college later" is a real possibility, not a guarantee — treat it as a benefit you verify, not a reason by itself to enlist.

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent one-pager — the structured path

What your student is considering: a path with built-in structure — military service, a registered apprenticeship, or a small-cohort training program — because they run best with clear expectations and a team.

Why this can be smart: structure is a legitimate success strategy, not a crutch. Apprenticeships pay while training; service can provide training, discipline, and education benefits afterward; cohort programs give a schedule and a group that notices. All three beat drifting.

The honest risks, by option: military service is a binding multi-year commitment with real risks — insist on conversations with veterans, not just recruiters, and a cooling-off period before any signature. Apprenticeship spots are competitive and take years — apply broadly, verify the sponsor's reputation. Any program promising guaranteed outcomes is waving a red flag; none can.

Questions worth deciding together: What is the exact commitment length, and what does leaving early cost? What did the veteran and the apprentice actually say? Which benefits have you both read at the official source (va.gov, apprenticeship.gov) rather than heard about? What's the fallback if the first application cycle says no?

Free thing to do together: visit one structured environment in person this month — an apprenticeship open house, a base if family can host you, or a small cohort classroom (ours tours free). Watching the structure beats imagining it.
