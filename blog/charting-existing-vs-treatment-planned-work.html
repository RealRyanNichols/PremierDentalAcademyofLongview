Existing vs. treatment-planned: the charting difference that trips up new assistants

Every chart tells two stories at once: what is already in the mouth, and what the dentist plans to do about it. New assistants mix those stories up constantly — and the mix-up isn't cosmetic. It denies claims, wrecks schedules, and confuses patients. Here's the clean way to think about it.

The two stories, defined

Existing is history: restorations, crowns, extractions, implants that are already in (or out of) the mouth when the patient sits down — work your office did years ago or work some other office did. Treatment-planned is future: what the dentist has diagnosed and proposed but hasn't done yet. Charting systems mark these differently — existing work in one notation or color, planned work in another — precisely so anyone opening the chart can tell instantly what is from what will be.

One boundary before anything else: the dentist diagnoses and treatment-plans; the assistant records. Charting existing work is observation. Entering planned work is transcribing the dentist's decision — never your own judgment about what a tooth needs.

A fictional patient, both stories

Meet "Marcus," a fictional new patient. The exam finds: a crown on #19 done five years ago elsewhere, an amalgam on #30, and decay on #3 for which the doctor plans a crown. Charted correctly, #19 and #30 are marked existing and #3 carries a planned crown. Now the schedule knows #3 needs appointments, the insurance coordinator knows #3 is the claim that's coming, and next year's hygienist knows the #19 crown wasn't your office's work. Flip one entry — mark the #3 crown as existing — and the plan silently vanishes: nothing gets scheduled, nothing gets billed, and the decay sits there.

Why insurance cares so much

The denial machine

Claims are checked against the chart's story. Bill for a crown the chart says already existed, and the payer reads it as billing for old work — denial. Mark planned work as existing, and coverage checks run against the wrong picture. Many "mystery" denials trace back to exactly this one charting distinction, which is why the claims workflow in Practice Pro makes you chart the story correctly before a claim will play out cleanly.
