What to wear to a dental assistant interview (and what to bring)

Nobody tells you this part. You finish your training, you land the interview, and then you stand in front of your closet at 10 p.m. wondering whether scrubs are the right call. Here is the plain answer, plus the folder that quietly does half the work for you.

The short answer: business casual, clean, and quiet

For a dental assistant interview, business casual is the safe center of the road. Think slacks or a simple skirt, a blouse or a collared shirt, closed-toe shoes you can stand in. Clean, pressed, and unremarkable. You are not trying to be the most stylish person in the room — you are trying to look like someone a dentist can picture in their operatory on Monday morning.

"Quiet" is the word that matters most. In dentistry, you work six inches from someone's face. Loud perfume, long nails, jangling bracelets, and a big necklace that swings forward when you lean over a patient all send the same signal: this person has not thought about the job yet. Leave them at home.

Should I wear scrubs?

Usually no — unless the office specifically tells you to, or tells you there will be a hands-on portion. If they say "wear scrubs," wear plain, solid-color scrubs in good shape and bring your own closed-toe shoes. If nobody says anything, business casual is the safer choice. It is always fine to call the office the day before and ask, "Is there anything you'd like me to wear or bring?" That question makes you look prepared, not unsure.

The head-to-toe checklist

Hair: pulled back and off your face, the way you would wear it chairside. This is a small detail that tells an experienced office you understand the work.

Nails: short, natural, clean. Long or artificial nails are a real infection-control conversation in a clinical setting, and an interview is not the moment to have it. Read more in infection control basics.

Scent: none. Not light — none. Patients in a dental chair are already sensitive, and so are the people you would be working beside all day.

Jewelry: a watch and simple studs. Nothing that dangles, catches, or has to be removed before you can work.

Shoes: closed-toe, clean, comfortable enough to stand in. If they ask you to walk back through the office, you want to move easily.

Tattoos and piercings: policies vary widely from office to office, and there is no single East Texas rule. The safe move for one interview is to keep facial jewelry minimal and cover what covers easily — then ask about their policy once you have an offer, so you know what daily life will look like.

What goes in the folder

Bring a plain folder. Inside it, five things — and they should be printed, not "on your phone."

1. Three copies of your resume. 2. Copies of your certificates and course completions. 3. Your reference list with names, roles, and phone numbers. 4. A short list of your own questions for them. 5. A pen and a small notepad.

Three copies sounds excessive until you walk into a room with the dentist, the office manager, and the lead assistant. Handing each of them a page instead of watching one copy get passed around changes the whole tone of the first two minutes. The working interview

Some offices will invite you back for a working interview — a half day or a day where you are actually in the operatory. If that happens, the dress code changes: plain scrubs, closed-toe non-slip shoes, hair back, nails short, no scent. Bring your own safety glasses if you have them. Ask ahead of time whether the day is paid and what hours they expect; that is a normal, professional question, and a good office will answer it plainly.

During a working interview, nobody expects you to be fast. They are watching whether you keep your area clean, whether you ask before you guess, and whether you can be told something once. That is it. If you trained on real office workflows — charting, tray setups, the schedule — you will feel the difference here, because the room will not be entirely new to you.

What the outfit cannot do

Dressing well gets you past the first ten seconds. What gets you the job is the next forty minutes. Know the procedures you can assist with, be honest about what you have not done yet, and have two or three questions ready about the office itself — how many operatories, what software they run, who you would be working under. Our post on common dental assistant interview questions and answers covers the ones that come up most.

And be honest about pay expectations rather than guessing. Look at the ranges on our East Texas RDA salary page before you go, so the number you say out loud is a number you actually thought about.

One thing people forget

Get the name and spelling of everyone you meet, and send a short thank-you email that same evening. Two or three sentences. Mention one specific thing from the conversation. In a small market like ours, offices talk to each other — being the person who followed up well is worth more than any outfit.
