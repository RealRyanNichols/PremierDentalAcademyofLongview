For parents: supporting an adult child who wants a trade career

Some of the best questions we get on the phone aren't from students at all. They're from a mom or a dad, doing quiet research before they say anything, trying to figure out how to be helpful without being the reason their kid stops talking to them about it.

First, separate two different worries

When a grown kid says "I don't want to do four more years of school, I want to do something with my hands," most parents feel two things at once and the two get tangled:

Worry one: is this a real path? That's a question about the field and the program, and it has answers you can actually go check.

Worry two: are they giving up on something? That's a question about them, and it usually has more to do with the story you were told about what a successful life looks like than with anything happening in front of you.

They need different handling. The first one deserves research, and you can be genuinely useful there. The second one deserves a conversation where you mostly listen — and the fastest way to turn a good conversation into a closed door is to answer the first worry out loud while you're actually feeling the second one.

The question that actually matters

Strip away the labels — trade, degree, certificate, program — and the useful question about any training is small and concrete: at the end of this, will they be able to do something specific that somebody pays for, and can the school show you how they teach it?

That question cuts cleanly. A program that can walk you through the room, the equipment, the weekly schedule, and what a student does in week one versus week ten is answering it. A program that mostly talks about opportunity and demand and your future — without showing you a room — is not.

It also keeps you out of a comparison you can't win. Whether a credential is "worth it" against a four-year degree depends on the person, the field, the cost, the local job market, and a dozen things nobody on the internet knows about your family. What you can evaluate is whether this particular program, in this particular town, teaches a real skill in a way you can see with your own eyes.

How to help without taking over

Go with them, don't go instead of them. If you call the school, ask questions and then hand the phone over. The person who is going to sit in that room for twelve weeks needs to have had the conversation themselves, because the first hard week is a lot harder if they're doing it to satisfy you.

Ask what they're worried about before you say what you're worried about. Most people considering a career change or a first credential have already argued both sides in their own head at 2 a.m. If you lead with your concern, you're arguing with a conclusion they already reached. If you ask first, you'll usually find they've thought of the same objections — and you become someone helping them think instead of one more person to convince.

Be useful on logistics. Transportation, childcare, a quiet place to study, covering a shift once a week — those are concrete forms of support and they're often the actual difference between finishing and not. They also don't require you to have an opinion about the career.

Let money be a plan, not a verdict. "How would we pay for this?" is a good question. "You can't afford that" is the end of a conversation. If you only do one thing

Go on the tour together. Twenty minutes standing in a room where students are working answers more than a week of reading — for both of you. It's free, there's no pitch, and if the place isn't right you'll know before anyone has spent a dollar.

What a short credential is — and what it isn't

A twelve-week program isn't a shortcut around learning; it's a narrower target. Instead of a broad education across many subjects, it teaches one job's worth of skills and expects you to be able to do them at the end. That's a fair trade for some people and the wrong trade for others, and which one your kid is depends on what they want out of the next few years — not on which option sounds more impressive at a family dinner.

The other honest thing to say: finishing a program is not the same as being licensed or registered by the state. In dental assisting in Texas, those are two separate tracks — a school's certificate of completion on one side, and the Texas State Board of Dental Examiners' registration requirements on the other. Any program that blurs that line is one to be careful with. We wrote the whole distinction out in accredited, licensed, or registered?, and the state's own rules are always at tsbde.texas.gov.
