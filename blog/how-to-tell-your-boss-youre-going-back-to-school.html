How to tell your boss you're going back to school

Most people put this conversation off for weeks. Then they rehearse the worst version of it in their head at 2 a.m. Here's the calmer way to do it — and why the version you're dreading usually isn't the one you get.

First: you may not owe anyone this conversation

Let's start there, because a lot of people skip it. What you do with your own time and your own money is your business. If your class days don't overlap with your shifts at all, you are allowed to simply go to school and say nothing until you have something to say.

But most people do want to have the talk, for good reasons: they need a schedule adjustment, they don't want it to come out sideways from a coworker, or they'd rather leave on good terms when the time comes. If any of those are you, keep reading.

The single most useful reframe

You are not asking permission. You are giving notice of a schedule and offering a plan. Those two sentences sound completely different across a desk, and the second one is the one that gets a yes.

Know your actual schedule before you open your mouth

Nothing sinks this conversation faster than "I think it's some mornings?" Walk in knowing exactly what you're asking for. Our in-person program runs about 12 weeks, and cohorts meet two or three days a week depending on which one you join — the Monday/Wednesday/Friday track or the Tuesday/Thursday track. Classes are daytime. For exact hours, call or text (903) 913-6444 — Amanda will tell you straight, and you'll want that in hand before you talk to anyone at work.

Then check it against your job. Two questions:

  1. Do my class days actually conflict with my shifts? Sometimes they don't at all.
  2. If they do, what's the smallest change that fixes it? A shift swap. Moving to evenings or weekends. Dropping from five days to four. Going part-time for one quarter of a year.

Bring the smallest fix, not the biggest ask. See working while you're in dental assistant school for how students around here actually stack the two, and check upcoming start dates so you're proposing real weeks.

Timing: earlier than you think, later than panic

The sweet spot is usually two to four weeks before your first class day. Early enough that a manager can build a schedule around it without scrambling; late enough that you're certain you're going. Don't announce an intention — announce a decision with a date on it.

Pick a calm moment. Not the middle of a rush, not right after something went wrong, not by text. A short scheduled conversation reads as respect, and respect is most of what you're trading for here.

Do not lead with the reason you're leaving

Even if the reason is "this job is going nowhere." That's true, it's yours, and it does absolutely nothing for you in this room. Lead with the schedule and the plan. Save the honest career reflection for people who love you.

There's no single right wording — there's the wording that sounds like you. Pick an opener, a schedule line, an offer, and a close, and you'll have something you can actually say out loud in the truck before you walk in.

What to do with the three reactions you might get

Supportive. More common than people expect, especially from managers who've watched someone leave for something better before. Say thank you, put the schedule in writing that same day, and be exactly as reliable as you promised. This is how you keep a reference.

Neutral or transactional. "Okay, send me your availability." That's a fine outcome. Send it within the hour, in writing, with the dates spelled out.

Cold or discouraging. It happens. Sometimes it's about staffing, sometimes it's about them. Don't argue and don't defend your choice — you don't have to win the conversation, you just have to leave it with your plan intact. Then go be good at your job while you train.

Protect the bridge

East Texas is smaller than it looks. The manager you're talking to today may know somebody at an office you'll interview with in a few months. Finish strong, give real notice when the day comes, and leave people glad they hired you. That costs you nothing and it's worth a lot.

And the other conversation

The one at home is usually harder than the one at work. If you haven't had it yet, telling your family you're going back to school walks through it, and going to dental assistant school with kids at home is written for the parents doing this on top of everything else.

Twelve weeks is short. You will be explaining your class schedule to people for about a quarter of a year, and then you'll be explaining a new job instead. That's the whole trade.

Have the conversation with a real start date in hand.
