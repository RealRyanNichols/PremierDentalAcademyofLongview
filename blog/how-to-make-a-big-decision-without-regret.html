How to make a big decision without regretting it: a working method

Here's what the research on regret keeps finding, and what your own grandmother could have told you: people regret the things they didn't try far more often than the things they tried and got wrong. Which means the goal of a big decision isn't to be certain — it's to decide cleanly enough that either outcome leaves you at peace. There's a method for that.

Step one: sort reversible from irreversible

Most decisions that feel enormous are actually reversible, and reversible decisions deserve far less agony than we give them. Taking a 12-week course? Reversible — worst case, you spent twelve weeks learning it's not for you. Moving towns? Mostly reversible. Six figures of student debt? NOT reversible — that one deserves the agony. Sort honestly and give each decision only the weight its reversibility earns. Most people agonize over reversible choices and sleepwalk into irreversible ones; flipping that habit is half the method.

Why drift is the real regret machine

Not deciding IS deciding

Skip the decision date and the decision still gets made — by default, by inertia, by whoever else's plans fill the space. That's how people wake up three years into a life they never chose. When people say 'I regret not trying,' they're describing drift, not a decision. The method above exists to make sure that whatever happens, it happened on purpose.

When the decision is a career change

The method applies with extra tools available: test the field cheaply before committing (our free fit quiz and practice exam are ten-minute tests, a tour is a thirty-minute one), price the real cost including the money math, and remember the reversibility sort: twelve weeks of training is a profoundly reversible bet compared to staying five more years somewhere that's already answered your 10-minute test.
