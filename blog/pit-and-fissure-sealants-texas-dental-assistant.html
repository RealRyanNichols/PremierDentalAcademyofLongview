Sealants, Polishing and Scope: What a Texas RDA Is Trained To Do

People ask us what a registered dental assistant is allowed to do in Texas. That is two questions wearing one coat — what the training covers, and what the state permits — and only one of them is ours to answer.

Two questions, and only one is ours

Nearly every week somebody asks some version of this: can a dental assistant place sealants in Texas? Can I polish? What do I need to be allowed to do the fun stuff?

Fair question. But it is really two, and they have different owners.

Question one — what does the training cover? That is ours. We can tell you exactly what you will learn, practice and be graded on.

Question two — what are you permitted to perform, under what supervision, with what credential? That belongs to the Texas State Board of Dental Examiners. Their rules are theirs to set, and they change.

We are not going to print a list of permitted duties here. Not because it is a secret, but because a blog post is the worst possible place to learn a rule that can be amended after the post is written. Anything you read about scope on any school's website — ours included — should be confirmed against the board before you act on it. That is not us dodging. That is the correct habit, and it is one you will use for your entire career.

So what does the training actually cover?

Here is the honest answer to the half we own. Across about twelve weeks, an entry-level dental assisting program covers a fairly consistent set of ground:

Chairside procedures. How a restorative appointment runs start to finish, what gets set out, when, and what the dentist is going to want next before they ask for it. This is where four-handed dentistry lives.

Preventive procedures — the family sealants and coronal polishing belong to. You learn what they are for, how the tooth surface has to be prepared, what a good result looks like and what a bad one looks like, and why the difference matters to a patient five years later.

Radiology. Positioning, technique, patient and operator safety, and what a diagnostic image looks like versus one you are going to have to retake.

Infection control. Standard precautions and the clean-to-dirty flow of an operatory and a sterilization area. Covered more fully in infection control basics.

Anatomy and charting. Tooth numbering, surfaces, existing versus needed treatment, and writing it down so the next person can read it.

The business side. Scheduling, clinical notes, insurance claims. The part nobody advertises and every office cares about.

Sealants and polishing, in plain English

Since those two come up most, here is what they actually are, minus the regulatory question.

A pit-and-fissure sealant is a thin coating flowed into the grooves on the chewing surface of a back tooth. Those grooves are narrower than a toothbrush bristle, which is exactly why decay likes them. Filling them in gives the brush a flat surface to work with. It is a small procedure with an outsized payoff, most often done for kids, and doing it well is mostly about a clean, genuinely dry surface — which is harder to hold than it sounds on a wiggly seven-year-old.

Coronal polishing is the removal of soft deposits and stain from the crown — the part of the tooth you can see. It is not scaling, it is not a replacement for a cleaning, and the distinction matters. Done well it is comfortable and thorough; done carelessly it is neither, and the patient remembers.

Worth being clear about: diagnosis, treatment planning and the procedures reserved to a licensed dentist are a dentist's. An assistant supports the dentist; an assistant does not practice dentistry. Any program that blurs that line is doing you harm, not a favor.

If you are weighing which add-on credential to chase first, we wrote about that: coronal polishing or nitrous.

What the day actually looks like

Strip out the credential talk and the job is more ordinary and more human than the search results suggest. You are setting up rooms and breaking them down. You are taking images. You are seating people, a lot of whom are frightened and would rather be anywhere else, and talking them down without making a production of it. You are anticipating what the dentist needs. You are writing notes somebody can bill from. You are keeping the schedule from quietly falling apart by ten-thirty.

The clinical skills are the part people ask about. The composure is the part that gets you kept.

Certificate of completion is not state registration

This one trips up more people than any scope question, so let us be blunt about it.

When you finish our program, we issue a Premier Dental Academy Certificate of Completion. That is a school document. It says you did the work.

Becoming a Registered Dental Assistant in Texas is a separate thing entirely, handled by the state board, on their requirements and their timeline. No school registers you. No school can promise you will pass anything, and you should be suspicious of one that implies otherwise.

Two different documents, two different issuers, two different meanings. Our registration guide lays out how the pieces relate, and the board's site is where the current requirements live.

How to answer a scope question the right way

You will hit this in a real office someday — somebody will ask you to do something and you will not be sure. The habit that keeps you safe is boring and it works:

  1. Ask the dentist you work for. They carry the license the practice runs on and they have a direct interest in the answer being right.
  2. Check the board's current rules yourself, at tsbde.texas.gov. Not a forum, not a Facebook group, not a coworker's memory of a class in 2019.
  3. If it is still unclear, do not do it yet. Nobody has ever been fired for asking one more question. The other way around happens.

That is not timidity. An assistant who checks is an assistant a dentist can relax around, and that is worth more than any single skill on your list.
