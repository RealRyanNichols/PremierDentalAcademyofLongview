Train them or hire them experienced? An honest look for office owners

Every office owner has had this argument with themselves at 9 p.m. over a stack of résumés. The honest answer is that both paths work and both can go wrong — the question is which risks your office is better built to absorb. Here is the operations-level version, with no invented statistics.

The case for the experienced hire — genuinely

Sometimes experienced is simply right. If you're down an assistant today, production is bleeding and you need hands that already know a high-volume day. An experienced assistant brings pattern recognition no course teaches: they've seen the emergency walk-in, the difficult extraction, the doctor having an off day. If your office has no bandwidth to mentor — solo doc, packed columns, no senior assistant — hiring experienced is the responsible call, and we'll say that even though we run a school.

The case for training your own

The trade is ramp time for formability. A new graduate arrives without another office's habits — no "that's how we did it at my last job" friction, no shortcuts learned under a doctor with different standards. You form them to your sterilization rhythm, your charting expectations, your patient tone. People also tend to stay loyal to the office that gave them their first real chance — not a statistic, just a pattern most owners recognize. And the salary conversation typically starts at a different place with a first-year assistant than a five-year one; the trade-off is the productivity gap while they ramp.

How training-forward offices shrink the ramp

The ramp is the whole objection to new graduates — so the question becomes what shortens it. Two things, in our experience: a structured first week instead of "shadow whoever's closest," and graduates who have already practiced on office-style software. That second one is our entire teaching model — our students chart, schedule, and work claims in trainers built like real practice software before they ever stand in your op.
